METHODOLOGY NOTES: Census Bureau's "Estimated Median Age at First Marriage" (Table MS-2)
and the Hajnal Singulate Mean Age at Marriage (SMAM)
===================================================================================
Compiled from Census Bureau documentation, UN metadata, and demographic literature.

## CRITICAL DISTINCTION: TWO DIFFERENT METHODS

The Census Bureau's Table MS-2 uses a DIFFERENT method from SMAM, though both
rely on the same input data (cross-sectional proportions never-married by age).

------------------------------------------------------------------------
METHOD 1: CENSUS BUREAU'S INDIRECT MEDIAN (used in Table MS-2 for CPS data)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Source: Census Bureau CPS Subject Definitions; Shryock & Siegel (1971)
"The Methods and Materials of Demography", Vol. 1, pp. 291-296.

Official Census Bureau definition:
"The estimated median age at first marriage is an approximation derived
indirectly from tabulations of marital status and age. In computing this
median, several steps are involved:
  1. First, the expected proportion of young people who will ever marry
     during their lifetime is computed.
  2. Second, one-half of this expected proportion is calculated.
  3. And third, the current age of young people who are at this halfway
     mark is computed."

STEP-BY-STEP ALGORITHM:

Step 1: Tabulate proportion never-married (single) by age group from CPS.
  - Use 5-year age groups: 15-19, 20-24, 25-29, ..., 45-49, 50-54
  - Denote S_a = proportion never-married in age group a

Step 2: Estimate the proportion who will EVENTUALLY marry.
  - Use the proportion ever-married among the oldest age group (typically
    ages 45-54) as the estimate: P_ever_marry = 1 - S_{45-54}
  - This assumes that marriage after age 45-54 is negligible, so the
    observed ever-married proportion at these ages approximates the
    lifetime proportion who will ever marry.
  - Example from Census 2000: 90.6% for men, 92.5% for women.

Step 3: Compute one-half of P_ever_marry.
  - half_P = P_ever_marry / 2
  - This is the target: find the age at which half of those who will
    ever marry have already married.

Step 4: Find the age at which the cumulative proportion ever-married
  equals half_P by interpolation through the never-married schedule.
  - Convert the never-married proportions to ever-married: E_a = 1 - S_a
  - Walk through age groups from youngest to oldest.
  - Find the two adjacent age groups a_low and a_high where:
      E_{a_low} < half_P <= E_{a_high}
  - Linearly interpolate to find the exact age:
      median_age = a_low + width * (half_P - E_{a_low}) / (E_{a_high} - E_{a_low})
  - Shryock & Siegel originally described "osculatory interpolation"
    (smoother than linear), but in practice linear interpolation through
    the schedule is standard.

KEY ASSUMPTION: SYNTHETIC COHORT / STATIONARITY
  - The cross-sectional age-specific never-married proportions are treated
    as if they represent a single cohort's marriage trajectory over time.
  - This means: the proportion of 25-year-olds who are never-married in
    2020 is assumed to equal what today's 20-year-olds will look like at
    age 25 -- i.e., marriage patterns are assumed constant across cohorts.
  - The Census Bureau itself notes: "the method makes the assumption that
    younger cohorts will marry at the same age as older cohorts have in
    the past."
  - This assumption is VIOLATED whenever marriage timing is changing
    across generations (which it has been, continuously, since ~1960).

NOTE ON ACS vs CPS:
  - The ACS (since ~2008) asks directly about year of first marriage, so
    the ACS-based median age at first marriage (Table B12007) is a DIRECT
    estimate from reported ages at first marriage, not an indirect estimate.
  - The CPS indirect method is what produces Table MS-2's historical series.
  - The two methods produce slightly different estimates (e.g., 2020 CPS:
    men 30.5, women 28.1; 2020 ACS: men 29.7, women 28.3).


------------------------------------------------------------------------
METHOD 2: HAJNAL'S SINGULATE MEAN AGE AT MARRIAGE (SMAM)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

Source: Hajnal, J. (1953). "Age at Marriage and Proportions Marrying."
Population Studies, Vol. 7, No. 2, pp. 111-136.

WHAT IT COMPUTES:
  SMAM = the average number of years lived in the never-married state
  among those who marry before age 50.

  NOTE: This is a MEAN, not a MEDIAN. It is conceptually different from
  the Census Bureau's "estimated median age at first marriage."

THE LIFE TABLE ANALOGY:
  Hajnal's key insight: the cross-sectional proportion never-married at
  each age is analogous to l(x) in a life table -- the proportion
  "surviving" (remaining single) to age x.

  Just as in a life table:
    - e(0) = integral of l(x) dx / l(0) = mean age at death

  For marriage:
    - SMAM = integral of S(x) dx from 15 to 50, adjusted for those who
      never marry = mean age at first marriage among those who do marry

THE FORMULA:

  Given S_a = proportion never-married (single) in 5-year age group a:

  Step 1: Compute A = total person-years lived in the single state
          from age 15 to age 50.

    A = 5 * [S_{15-19} + S_{20-24} + S_{25-29} + S_{30-34} + S_{35-39} + S_{40-44} + S_{45-49}]

    (If using single-year data, A = sum of S_x from x=15 to x=49)

    Note: Everyone is single from age 0 to 15, contributing 15 person-years
    each. So total person-years from birth = 15 + A. But since we want
    age at marriage (not years lived single from birth), the formula
    accounts for this below.

  Step 2: Estimate B = proportion who remain permanently single
          (never marry by age 50).

    B = (S_{45-49} + S_{50-54}) / 2

    If S_{50-54} is not available, B = S_{45-49}.

  Step 3: Compute C = proportion who ever marry by age 50.

    C = 1 - B

  Step 4: Compute D = person-years lived single by those who NEVER marry
          (from age 15 to 50).

    D = 50 * B

    (These people contribute a full 50 person-years each to the single
    state between ages 0 and 50. But since we're measuring from age 15,
    it's really 35*B, but the standard formula uses the version below.)

  Step 5: Compute SMAM.

    SMAM = (15 + A - D) / C

    Equivalently (from Germán Rodríguez, Princeton):

    SMAM = 15 + (5 * sum(S_{15-19} ... S_{45-49}) - 35 * B) / C

    Which simplifies to:

    SMAM = [15 + A - 50*B] / (1 - B)

    Or equivalently:

    SMAM = [15 + A - 50*B] / C

WORKED EXAMPLE (Turkish Males 1990, from Princeton):
  s_50 = 0.0233 (2.33% remain permanently single)
  SMAM = 25.00 years


------------------------------------------------------------------------
ASSUMPTIONS AND LIMITATIONS (BOTH METHODS)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. STATIONARITY (SYNTHETIC COHORT ASSUMPTION):
   Both methods treat cross-sectional data as if it represents a cohort's
   lifecycle. The 30-year-olds and 25-year-olds in the same survey are
   different birth cohorts, but the method assumes their marriage patterns
   are identical. If marriage timing is shifting across cohorts (which it
   has been in the US since ~1960), this assumption is violated.

   Effect: When marriage age is RISING across cohorts, both methods
   OVERESTIMATE the true cohort median/mean because the older cohorts
   (who married younger) make it look like a higher proportion has married
   by each age than will actually be true for younger cohorts.

   Wait -- actually the opposite: when marriage age is rising, younger
   cohorts show HIGHER never-married proportions at each age than older
   cohorts did. The synthetic cohort method uses the young cohorts' high
   never-married rates for young ages and the old cohorts' low never-married
   rates for old ages. This creates a schedule where marriage looks like
   it happens LATER than it actually did for ANY real cohort. The indirect
   median will be HIGHER than any real cohort's median.

2. NO ADJUSTMENT FOR MORTALITY / SURVIVORSHIP BIAS:
   Neither method adjusts for differential mortality by marital status.
   If married people have lower mortality than never-married people
   (which is empirically true), then at older ages, the proportion
   never-married is slightly inflated (because more of the never-married
   died). This would slightly bias SMAM upward.

   Per the academia.edu paper on relaxing SMAM assumptions: "Relaxing
   mortality and migration assumptions leads to inflated SMAM estimates,
   potentially by up to six months, particularly in small populations
   with higher mortality rates."

3. NO ADJUSTMENT FOR MIGRATION:
   In populations with significant immigration/emigration, the age
   structure of never-married proportions reflects migration patterns,
   not just marriage patterns. "In populations with high levels of male
   emigration, SMAM becomes unreliable for estimating male age at
   marriage, necessitating correction methods for accuracy."

4. COHORT MIXING IS INHERENT, NOT "HANDLED":
   Neither method attempts to decompose the cross-sectional data into
   true cohort trajectories. The synthetic cohort assumption IS the
   method -- there is no correction for cohort mixing. That's the
   fundamental limitation.


------------------------------------------------------------------------
APPLICATION TO HEADSHIP DATA (INSTEAD OF MARRIAGE)
------------------------------------------------------------------------

The same methods can be applied to ANY absorbing-state transition measured
by current status:

  Replace: "proportion never married" at age a
  With:    "proportion never-been-a-householder" at age a (or whatever
            the relevant status is)

For SMAM-analog (mean age at first headship):
  - S_a = proportion who have NEVER been a householder at age a
  - A = 5 * sum of S_a across age groups
  - B = proportion who never become householders (from oldest age group)
  - SMAM_headship = (15 + A - 50*B) / (1 - B)

For Census-median-analog (median age at first headship):
  - Tabulate proportion who are NOT currently householders by age
  - Estimate proportion who will ever become householders (from oldest ages)
  - Find age where half of eventual householders have become householders
  - Interpolate

CRITICAL DIFFERENCE FOR HEADSHIP:
  - Marriage is (mostly) an absorbing state: once married, you're
    "ever-married" forever, even if divorced/widowed.
  - Headship is NOT an absorbing state: you can be a householder at 30,
    then not at 35 (moved back with parents, etc.), then again at 40.
  - This means "proportion who have NEVER been a householder" is not
    directly observable from a cross-section that only reports CURRENT
    status. You would need survey data on "have you EVER been a
    householder?" (which doesn't exist).
  - Using CURRENT headship status as a proxy is reasonable but
    introduces additional error from people cycling in and out of
    headship.
