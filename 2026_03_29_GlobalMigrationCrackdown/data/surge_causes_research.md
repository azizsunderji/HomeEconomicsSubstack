# What Caused the Synchronized Global Migration Surge of 2022-2023?

## Research compiled March 2026

---

## Executive Summary

Between 2022 and 2023, international migration to OECD countries surged to unprecedented levels: 6 million new permanent immigrants in 2022, rising to 6.5 million in 2023---both all-time records, and 28% above 2019 pre-pandemic levels. This surge was synchronized across virtually every major destination country: the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand, Germany, Netherlands, Ireland, and Portugal all hit record or near-record migration levels simultaneously.

The key analytical puzzle: the surge was **far larger than the pandemic shortfall**. If COVID simply suppressed migration in 2020-2021, you'd expect a catch-up effect returning levels to trend. Instead, 2022-2023 migration far exceeded what would have been "normal" even without COVID. Something structural changed.

The answer is not monocausal. At least seven factors converged simultaneously, which explains the synchronization:

1. **Pandemic backlog clearing** (necessary but insufficient---explains return to trend, not the overshoot)
2. **Extreme labor market tightness in destination countries** (the demand-side pull)
3. **Deliberate policy loosening by destination countries** (the enabler)
4. **India's educated-youth unemployment crisis** (the dominant supply-side push)
5. **Ukrainian displacement** (explains European surge, especially Germany)
6. **Student visa pathways as migration backdoors** (the mechanism)
7. **Social media and information effects** (the accelerant)

---

## 1. Pent-Up Demand / Visa Backlog Clearing

### The Evidence

- US visa issuances surged to 6.8 million in 2022 as consular capacity reopened. Non-immigrant visa issuances surpassed 10 million in 2023.
- The USCIS net backlog stood at 5+ million cases at end of FY 2022. By end of FY 2023, it was reduced to 4.3 million (down 15%)---the first backlog reduction in over a decade.
- Congress provided $275 million in appropriated funding in FY 2022 to accelerate processing.
- The State Department waived in-person interview requirements for green-card applicants who had been previously interviewed but couldn't travel during COVID.
- Employment-based adjustments of status jumped to 221,373 in 2022, vs. ~107,000 pre-pandemic, partly because unused family-sponsored visa slots were rolled over to employment-based categories.
- Australia had 369,000 people on bridging visas by September 2022---people stuck in-country awaiting visa processing.

### Assessment

**Explains maybe 40-50% of the surge**---the return-to-trend portion. Backlog clearing accounts for the initial rebound but not the overshoot. As Penn Wharton's analysis notes, F-1 student visas in 2023 were still ~3% below 2019 levels, and family-based LPR arrivals remained depressed. The categories that surged beyond trend (H-1B workers at +35% vs 2019, unauthorized crossings, temporary workers) require separate explanations.

**Does it explain synchronization?** Yes---every country simultaneously reopened consular processing and cleared backlogs, creating a coordinated release of pent-up demand.

**Does it explain exceeding the COVID shortfall?** No. The backlog clearing accounts for the snap-back, but the overshoot requires demand-side and supply-side explanations.

---

## 2. Labor Shortages in Destination Countries

### The Evidence

- **Goldman Sachs finding**: "Immigration drove most of the employment gains in Canada, New Zealand, Sweden, Germany and the UK in 2023, and added more than four million jobs in the US." (Cited in Al Jazeera, Dec 2025)
- **San Francisco Fed (Evgeniya Duzhak, Feb 2023)**: The pandemic created a shortfall of 2 million foreign working-age people in the US by 2021 relative to pre-pandemic trends (citing Peri and Zaiour 2022). The immigration slowdown from 2017-2021 tightened local labor markets, raising the vacancy-to-unemployment ratio by 5.5 percentage points.
- **Kansas City Fed**: The immigration rebound in 2022 brought the V-U ratio down almost 6 percentage points, acting as "a powerful catalyst in cooling overheated labor markets."
- Goldman Sachs estimated immigration reduced US wage growth by three-tenths of a percentage point overall, and at least double that in leisure and hospitality.
- By April 2024, the foreign-born labor force was ~900,000 larger than it would have been if it had simply grown at the 2000-2019 trend rate of 2.2% per year.

### The Mechanism

The post-COVID labor market was uniquely tight across all OECD countries simultaneously. This was driven by:
- Pandemic-era retirements (especially in healthcare, hospitality)
- Reduced domestic labor force participation
- Aging populations reducing working-age population growth (US fertility rate: 1.6 in 2024, well below 2.1 replacement)
- Strong fiscal-stimulus-driven demand recovery

This labor tightness created a **demand pull** that actively drew migrants. Employers lobbied for expanded visa programs, governments responded with policy loosening (see #3), and migrants responded to abundant job opportunities and high wages.

### Assessment

**This is the strongest single explanation for the overshoot.** Labor market tightness was a global phenomenon---every OECD country experienced it simultaneously due to synchronized COVID recovery, synchronized aging demographics, and synchronized stimulus-driven demand. This explains both the synchronization and the above-trend levels.

**Does it explain synchronization?** Yes---the pandemic hit all OECD economies simultaneously, and the fiscal-stimulus-driven recovery was also synchronized, creating simultaneous labor shortages.

**Does it explain exceeding the COVID shortfall?** Yes---the structural labor shortage was worse than the pandemic migration shortfall, creating demand for even more immigrants than would have been "normal."

## 3. Deliberate Policy Loosening by Destination Countries

### Country-by-Country Evidence

**Canada:**
- Set immigration targets of 431,645 permanent residents for 2022 (achieved---"the largest number of people ever welcomed in a year in Canadian history")
- 2023-2025 Immigration Levels Plan set targets of 410,000-505,000 for 2023, ramping to 442,500-550,000 by 2025
- In April 2022, allowed employers in accommodation and food services to hire up to 30% of workforce through Temporary Foreign Worker program for low-wage positions
- Temporary residents (students + work permit holders) ballooned from 1.7 million (Oct 2022) to 2.5 million (Oct 2023)---a near-doubling in two years
- Work permit holders with work permits only surged 152% over two years to exceed 1.3 million

**United Kingdom:**
- Post-Brexit points-based system (Jan 2021) opened work visas to non-EU nationals for the first time, replacing free movement
- Work visas issued in the 12 months ending June 2022 were ~double pre-pandemic levels (331,000 vs ~168,000 average for 2014-2019)
- Health and Care Worker visa created with fast-track processing, reduced fees, and exemption from Immigration Health Surcharge
- Health and Care visas: 350,000 granted in 2023 (including dependants) vs 118,000 skilled visas in other sectors
- Share of Skilled Worker visas going to health/care roles jumped from 58% (2022) to 75% (2023)
- Government set explicit target of 600,000 international students by 2030---hit it in 2022, eight years early
- Net migration peaked at 944,000 in the year ending March 2023

**Australia:**
- Borders reopened February 21, 2022, after nearly two years of closure
- Net overseas migration hit 518,000 in 2022-23---a record
- Three out of every four arrivals were on temporary visas (554,000)
- Extended pandemic-era temporary employment visas (subclass 408) and expanded graduate visa eligibility
- No caps on temporary migration during the surge period

**United States:**
- Title 42 paradox: CDC border expulsion policy created a perverse incentive for repeat crossings. Nearly half of 1.8 million expulsions were the same people being apprehended and expelled back to Mexico multiple times. Recidivism surged from 7% (FY 2019) to 27% (FY 2021). Individual migrants made dozens of attempts.
- CBP One app authorized for asylum applications when Title 42 ended (May 2023)
- Employment-based visa rollovers from unused family allocations
- State Department interview waivers

**New Zealand:**
- Record net migration gain of 135,500 in the year ended October 2023

**Netherlands:**
- Immigration jumped 60% in 2022, to 403,108---above pre-pandemic levels even excluding Ukrainian refugees

**Ireland:**
- 141,600 immigrants in year to April 2023 (16-year high, +31% vs prior year)
- 68,000 Ukrainians arrived under EU Temporary Protection Directive

**Portugal:**
- Foreign residents doubled over a decade to 800,000 in 2022
- New immigrants in 2022: 121,000 (+29% vs 2021)
- Digital nomad visa launched October 2022

### Assessment

**Policy loosening was the enabler, not the root cause.** Governments loosened policy *because* of labor shortages. But the loosening created feedback loops: once the door was opened wider, migration flows exceeded what policymakers anticipated. Canada's temporary resident explosion is the clearest example---the government set permanent resident targets but lost control of temporary pathways.

**Does it explain synchronization?** Partially---multiple countries loosened policy simultaneously in response to the same labor market pressures. But each country made distinct policy choices (UK: health care visas; Canada: temporary worker expansion; Australia: border reopening).

**Does it explain exceeding the COVID shortfall?** Yes---in most cases, policies were more permissive than pre-pandemic settings, not merely restored to pre-pandemic levels.

## 4. India's Educated-Youth Unemployment Crisis

### The Data

- India became the **#1 source country** for OECD migration, displacing China (OECD Migration Outlook 2025)
- 1.3 million Indian students abroad as of 2024 (second-largest sending country globally; surpassed China as #1 source for US students in 2023/24 with 331,602 enrolled)
- Indian diaspora: 18.5 million in 2024, up from 6.5 million in 1990
- India's net migration in 2023: -979,179 people

### Push Factors

**Graduate unemployment crisis:**
- Unemployment among 15-25 year-olds with degrees: ~40%. Among 25-29: ~20%.
- In 2022, unemployment rate among educated youth was 18.4% (secondary or higher) vs 3.4% for those who can't read or write---9x higher for graduates
- Two-thirds of India's unemployed youth are educated---double the share from 2000 (35.2% then, 66% now)
- ILO estimates graduate unemployment around 30%
- India Skills Report 2025: only 55% of Indian graduates deemed employable

**Structural mismatch:**
- The 5 original IITs received ~450,000 applicants competing for fewer than 10,000 undergraduate spots
- India's tertiary gross enrollment jumped from 6% (1995) to 33% (2023)---education expanded far faster than quality jobs
- GDP per capita: $2,481 (2023), vs vastly higher wages in destination countries

**Destination breakdown of Indian students:**
- Canada: 427,085 students (2023)---40%+ of all Canadian study permits
- US: 331,602 (2023/24)
- Australia: 163,450 (2023/24)
- UK: 126,580 (2022/23)
- Indians received nearly two-thirds of H-1B visas (FY 2023)
- ~80% of Indian students in the US study STEM or business (43% math/computer science, 25% engineering, 11% business)
- ~60% study at graduate level (predominantly master's)

### Assessment

**India is the single most important supply-side driver.** The simultaneous surge in Indian migration to Canada, US, UK, and Australia---all at once---explains a huge share of the synchronized global pattern. India's educated youth unemployment crisis is structural, not cyclical: it predates COVID and was not created by the pandemic. But the pandemic created conditions (reopened borders + labor shortages + expanded visa pathways) that allowed a massive release of pent-up emigration pressure from India.

**Does it explain synchronization?** Yes---India is a single source feeding into multiple destination countries simultaneously. When all destinations opened up at once after COVID, Indian migration surged to all of them at once.

**Does it explain exceeding the COVID shortfall?** Yes---Indian emigration pressure was building throughout the 2010s as the graduate unemployment crisis worsened. The post-COVID opening didn't just release pent-up demand from 2020-2021; it released a decade of building emigration pressure.

## 5. Ukrainian Displacement

### The Data

- 4.8 million Ukrainians resettled across Europe by June 2022; 5.1 million by June 2025
- Germany's net migration hit 1.5 million in 2022---highest since records began in 1950---with Ukrainians making up 41% of all immigrants
- Germany became the EU country with the highest number of Ukrainian refugees (1.1 million by end of 2024)
- Netherlands: 108,000+ Ukrainian refugees entered in 2022 (significant driver of the 110% increase in net migration)
- Ireland: 68,000 Ukrainians arrived under EU Temporary Protection Directive

### Characteristics

- 72% of adult Ukrainian refugees in Germany had university degrees (BAMF)
- Majority were women, often with children
- Many intended to return home, limiting long-term labor market integration
- Ukrainians eased labor shortages across European sectors, potentially reducing demand for additional foreign workers from other origins

### Assessment

**Explains the European surge almost entirely for 2022, but not the Anglo-Saxon countries.** Ukrainian displacement accounts for Germany's record, much of the Netherlands' surge, and a significant chunk of Ireland's numbers. But it doesn't explain the surges in Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand, or the US.

**Does it explain synchronization?** Only for European countries. The Anglo-Saxon surge requires different explanations.

**Does it explain exceeding the COVID shortfall?** For European countries, yes---Ukraine represents a genuinely new flow that didn't exist pre-pandemic. For non-European countries, no.

## 6. Student Visa Pathways as Migration Backdoors

### The Evidence

**UK (most explicit):**
- In 2019, government set a target of 600,000 international students by 2030. Hit it in 2022.
- Universities charge overseas students up to triple domestic fees---creating a financial incentive to recruit aggressively
- The percentage of non-EU visa holders on study visas transitioning to a different visa type after two years increased from 17% (22,000) for YE June 2020 arrivals to **62% (292,000)** of YE June 2023 arrivals
- Government estimates ~500,000 recent international graduates stayed after visas expired
- Dependants' visas hit 135,000 in 2023---each Health and Care worker brought on average one family member
- Dependants accounted for 48% of non-EU work immigration (up from 37% in 2019)

**Canada:**
- Study permit holders with or without work permits accounted for 46% of the increase in temporary foreign workers from 2011-2021
- Post-Graduation Work Permit (PGWP) program was a major driver---47% of temporary worker increases came from the International Mobility Program, mostly PGWPs
- 68% of Indian students worked post-study; 40% sought permanent residence pathways

**Australia:**
- Three out of four arrivals in 2022-23 were on temporary visas
- More than half of temporary visa holders were international students (283,000)
- Students transitioning to temporary graduate visas as a step toward permanent residence
- Peter McDonald (University of Melbourne): "Very little of the increase in net migration across these two years was due to the granting of permanent resident visas"---it was almost entirely temporary visa holders (students + working holidaymakers)

### Assessment

**The student pathway was the primary mechanism through which the surge operated.** Governments didn't increase permanent migration caps by nearly as much as actual migration surged. The explosion happened in temporary categories---especially student visas---which serve as de facto labor migration and permanent migration pathways. This is why the surge surprised policymakers: they were watching permanent immigration targets while temporary categories ballooned unchecked.

**Does it explain synchronization?** Yes---all major English-speaking destination countries (US, UK, Canada, Australia) had generous post-study work rights that attracted students from the same source countries (primarily India). When all these pathways reopened simultaneously, the same pool of students flooded into all of them at once.

**Does it explain exceeding the COVID shortfall?** Yes---student pathways grew far beyond pre-pandemic levels because they were being used as labor migration routes, not just education routes.

## 7. Social Media and Information Effects

### The Evidence

- TikTok became a new entry point for irregular migration information. The platform's recommendation algorithm repeatedly suggests migration content to people who aren't actively searching for it, expanding reach beyond traditional migration networks.
- Chinese migration to the US via the southern border surged ~10x: from ~900 encounters in December 2022 to ~6,000 in December 2023, driven by social media posts offering practical guides and celebrating the journey through Ecuador to the US-Mexico border.
- Criminal organizations used WhatsApp to coordinate transit logistics in real time across language barriers.
- "Migrant TikTok" flourished as a largely unchecked corner of the platform with videos about migration from Latin America to the US, including content from smugglers offering services.

### Assessment

**A genuine accelerant, but not a root cause.** Social media reduced information barriers and made migration routes more legible to potential migrants who wouldn't have had access to this information through traditional network channels. The 10x surge in Chinese migration via the southern border is the strongest evidence of social media's causal impact. But social media doesn't explain why labor markets were tight or why policy was loosened---it explains why the response to those conditions was faster and larger than in previous migration episodes.

**Does it explain synchronization?** Somewhat---social media is global and transmits information about multiple destination countries simultaneously.

**Does it explain exceeding the COVID shortfall?** Partially---by lowering information barriers, social media likely increased the pool of potential migrants beyond what traditional network effects would have produced.

## 8. Was the Surge Actually Just "Normal" Growth Relative to COVID Lows?

### The Evidence Against (i.e., it was NOT just normal)

- OECD: 2023 migration was 28% above 2019 levels (not just back to trend)
- US: CBO estimated 2.7 million immigrants in 2022, 3.3 million in 2023, vs 900,000 annual average in the 2010s---roughly 3x the pre-pandemic norm
- US: Net unauthorized immigration surged from 17,000 in 2020 to 2.2 million in 2023
- US: Foreign-born labor force was ~900,000 larger by April 2024 than it would have been growing at the 2000-2019 trend rate
- US: H-1B workers reached 440,000 in 2023, over one-third above pre-pandemic levels
- US: Nonimmigrant admissions rose to 4.2 million in 2023, exceeding the pre-pandemic peak of 3.75 million (2019) by over 10%
- Canada: population grew by 1.05 million in 2022 and 1.2 million in 2023, overwhelmingly from immigration---far above historical norms
- UK: Net migration peaked at 944,000 (year ending March 2023) vs pre-pandemic norms of ~200,000-300,000
- Australia: Peter McDonald (University of Melbourne) described 2022-23 as "the biggest two-year population surge in Australia's history"

### The Evidence For (i.e., it was partly catch-up)

- Australia: McDonald characterized it as "temporary catch-up, not structural change" and predicted net migration would "return to pre-pandemic levels as the number of migrant departures ticks upwards again"
- The timing mechanism is real: during COVID, many temporary migrants who would normally have departed were stuck in-country. When borders reopened, new arrivals entered while previous cohorts hadn't yet departed, creating a temporary bulge.
- Some categories (F-1 student visas, family-based green cards) were still below 2019 levels in 2023, suggesting catch-up hadn't completed in all channels.

### Assessment

**The surge was clearly above trend, not just catch-up.** Even the most conservative reading of the data shows 2022-2023 migration exceeded pre-pandemic norms by 20-300% depending on the country and metric. The catch-up mechanism was real and explains part of the early 2022 surge, but by 2023 the levels were far above any plausible trend line. The Wharton analysis is persuasive: some categories (employment-based immigration, unauthorized crossings) structurally exceeded pre-pandemic levels, while others (family-based, students in the US) remained below---suggesting the overshoot was driven by specific demand-side and supply-side factors, not a general catch-up.

## Synthesis: Why Did the Surge Happen?

The 2022-2023 global migration surge was the product of **seven factors converging simultaneously**:

| Factor | Explains return to trend? | Explains overshoot? | Explains synchronization? |
|--------|--------------------------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Backlog clearing | Yes | No | Yes |
| Labor market tightness | Yes | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Policy loosening | Yes | **Yes** | Partially |
| India push factors | No | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Ukrainian displacement | No (Europe only) | **Yes** (Europe) | No (Europe only) |
| Student visa backdoors | Yes | **Yes** | **Yes** |
| Social media | No | Partially | Partially |

### The most important insight: the overshoot was demand-driven

The pandemic didn't just create a temporary migration pause. It created a **structural labor shortage** in every OECD economy simultaneously, driven by:
- Early retirements
- Reduced labor force participation
- Accelerating demographic aging
- Strong fiscal-stimulus-driven demand

This labor shortage was more intense than the migration shortfall. Destination countries didn't just need to replace the immigrants who didn't come during COVID---they needed *more* immigrants than they would have needed even without COVID. The pandemic accelerated pre-existing demographic trends (aging, declining fertility) that were already increasing structural demand for immigrant labor.

### Why India?

India was the dominant source country because it had a massive, building pool of educated young people who couldn't find adequate employment domestically. India's graduate unemployment crisis had been building throughout the 2010s as tertiary enrollment tripled (6% to 33%) without corresponding job creation. When destination countries simultaneously opened doors wider after COVID, India's pent-up emigration pressure surged through every available channel---student visas to Canada, H-1B visas to the US, health care worker visas to the UK, skilled worker visas to Australia.

### Why student visas?

The student visa pathway was the primary mechanism because:
1. Permanent immigration caps were not raised nearly as much as temporary visa pathways
2. Student visas were the least controlled channel---universities had financial incentives to recruit, governments set targets for student numbers but didn't anticipate post-study transitions
3. Post-study work rights (UK Graduate Route, Canada PGWP, Australia graduate visa) turned student visas into de facto labor migration pathways
4. The UK data is stark: 62% of 2023 student arrivals transitioned to other visa types within two years
