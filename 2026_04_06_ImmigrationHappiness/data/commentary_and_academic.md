# Commentary and Academic Survey: Immigration and National Happiness

## Substacker/Commentator Analysis

### Matthew Yglesias (Slow Boring)

**Article 1: "What the Research Really Says About Immigration Politics"**
- Date: September 17, 2025
- URL: https://www.slowboring.com/p/what-the-research-really-says-about
- Core argument: Political science research on "accommodation" of populist-right immigration views is narrower than commonly understood. Mainstream parties adopting harsher anti-immigration rhetoric does not reduce the vote share of populist-right parties, due to salience effects. Immigration is politically costly but via "thermostatic backlash to policy change," not because immigration inherently harms wellbeing.
- Key quotes:
  - "Mainstream parties adopting harsher anti-immigration rhetoric does not reduce the vote share of populist-right parties."
  - "A large minority of the public agrees with them about immigration and crime."
  - "The findings are consistent with taking a wide range of moderate positions."
- Data cited: UK 2024 Labour gained 211 seats (201 to 412) despite populist-right surge; German data showing median voter positioning on immigration far-right of established parties' MPs.

**Article 2: "Saving Openness to Immigration"**
- Date: August 10, 2021
- URL: https://www.slowboring.com/p/immigration-openness
- Core argument: Immigration is America's critical competitive advantage, but political backlash over asylum processing threatens it. Backlash is not economically motivated but stems from loss-of-control perceptions. Less-educated immigrants provoke "much more political backlash than skilled ones."
- Key quotes:
  - "Most of America's billion-dollar startups have at least one immigrant founder."
  - "Uncontrolled flows of migrants freak people out."
- Data cited: Even George Borjas concedes immigration has net benefits for native-born American workers.
- Relevance to happiness: Yglesias frames the issue as political perception rather than measurable wellbeing decline. He notes that the research on intergroup contact "really depends on whether the contact is positive-sum or neutral or negative-sum," with neutral contact often increasing prejudice.

### Noah Smith (Noahpinion)

- Article: "The Siren Song of Homogeneity" (April 2017)
- URL: http://noahpinionblog.blogspot.com/2017/04/the-siren-song-of-homogeneity.html
- Core argument: The academic case for homogeneity producing higher trust/happiness is unresolved and weaker than proponents claim. Short-term diversity may create adjustment friction, but no clear evidence demonstrates permanent harm to social cohesion or happiness.
- Key quotes:
  - On Putnam: "The effect sizes in diversity-reduces-trust research are 'extremely small' according to Bryan Caplan's analysis of Putnam's work—insufficient to justify major policy changes."
  - On Japan: "Despite Japan's ethnic homogeneity... relatively low levels of interpersonal trust—historically lower than America's."
  - "Only 7% of Americans say diversity makes the country worse (versus 31-36% in European nations)."
- Data cited:
  - Southern California study: diversity associated with decreased crime and higher home values
  - Italian-Americans reached 40%+ outmarriage by early 1960s
  - Random assignment studies: white college students assigned Black roommates became more supportive of diversity
- Relevance: Smith proposes inverting the causal arrow—rather than homogeneity producing trust, institutions creating trust may produce homogeneity through intermarriage and redefinition of racial categories. Cites Putnam's own conclusion: "In the long run, however, successful immigrant societies have overcome such fragmentation by creating new, cross-cutting forms of social solidarity."

### Tyler Cowen (Marginal Revolution / The Free Press)

- Article: "Why 'Humane' Immigration Policy Ends in Cruelty" (The Free Press)
- URL: https://www.thefp.com/p/tyler-cowen-why-humane-immigration-policy-ends-in-cruelty
- Core argument: Immigration policy presents a fundamental dilemma: "every workable immigration policy, over enough time, offends liberal instincts or public opinion—often both." Societies swing between compassion and coercive control.
- Key quotes:
  - "The enforcement of immigration restrictions has to become more oppressive and more unpleasant as time passes."
  - The alternative—allowing immigration to increase unchecked—risks voter backlash that empowers "anti-immigrant parties—which are illiberal on many other issues as well."
- On social trust: Cowen acknowledges evidence that immigration reduces social trust in the short run, but argues it depends on implementation. He accepts Putnam's neighborhood-level research but emphasizes longer-run benefits. His number one recommendation for boosting growth is much more high-skilled immigration.
- Data cited: Migration can increase wages 260% for a Peruvian, 350% for a Filipino, 700% for a Haitian.

### Ezra Klein

- No single article directly on immigration and happiness identified. Klein has engaged with the Putnam tension—between diversity's effects on social trust and pro-immigration positions—but primarily through podcast discussions and book-length treatment of polarization. His work frames immigration attitudes as part of identity-based political polarization rather than a direct wellbeing question.

### Derek Thompson

- No single article directly linking immigration and happiness found. Thompson has covered happiness economics and immigration demographics separately. His "Plain English" podcast had a "Happiness Week" series, but specific immigration-happiness crossover was not identified in accessible content.

## Academic and Institutional Research

### Putnam (2007) — "E Pluribus Unum: Diversity and Community in the Twenty-first Century"
- Source: Scandinavian Political Studies, Johan Skytte Prize Lecture
- URL: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9477.2007.00176.x
- Finding: In ethnically diverse neighborhoods, residents of all races "hunker down"—trust (even of one's own race) is lower, altruism and community cooperation rarer, friends fewer. "Diversity, at least in the short run, seems to bring out the turtle in all of us."
- Methodology: Social Capital Community Benchmark Survey, ~30,000 Americans in 41 communities (2000)
- Key nuance: Putnam himself is optimistic long-term: "In the long run, however, successful immigrant societies have overcome such fragmentation by creating new, cross-cutting forms of social solidarity and more encompassing identities."
- Relevance: The foundational paper on the diversity-trust relationship. Widely cited by both immigration skeptics and proponents (with different emphases on short vs. long run).

### O'Connor (2020) — "The effect of immigration on natives' well-being in the European Union"
- Source: Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, Volume 180
- URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0167268120303747
- Finding: **No statistically significant effects** of immigration on natives' overall well-being across 28 European countries, 1990-2017. The null result holds for EU-origin immigrants, non-EU-origin immigrants, refugee populations, and across native subgroups (elderly, poorly educated).
- Methodology: Multiple econometric approaches including instrumental variable regressions; life satisfaction from Eurobarometer surveys; immigrant stock data from United Nations.
- Relevance: The most comprehensive null finding—immigration simply does not move the needle on European native life satisfaction, whether measured by immigrant origin, type, or native demographic.

### Helliwell, Bonikowska, & Shiplett (2016) — "Migration as a Test of the Happiness Set Point Hypothesis"
- Source: NBER Working Paper No. 22601
- URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w22601
- Finding: Life satisfaction scores among immigrants to Canada "mimic those of other Canadians rather than those in their source countries." Contradicts the happiness set-point hypothesis.
- Methodology: Comparison of immigrant life satisfaction with source-country populations, using Statistics Canada and Gallup data, covering immigrants from ~100 source countries.
- Relevance: Demonstrates that happiness responds to environmental circumstances, not just personality—immigrants converge toward host country norms. For the UK, immigrants from 70 countries averaged 7.57 life satisfaction vs. 7.65 for overall UK population (origin countries averaged 6.24).

### Brockmann & Fernandez-Urbano (2026) — "Females Welcome! When immigrants make the native population happy"
- Source: Journal of Economic Psychology, Volume 113 (March 2026)
- URL: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167487025000868
- Finding: Female immigration correlates with **increased** native life satisfaction; male immigration correlates with **decreased** native life satisfaction. Pattern persists across both male and female native respondents. During economic crises, the gender disparity diminishes.
- Methodology: Fixed-effects panel models, regression discontinuity design, and difference-in-differences specification; longitudinal data from Germany and Catalonia (Spain).
- Relevance: One of the first papers to disaggregate immigration's happiness effect by immigrant gender—suggests composition matters, not just volume.

### World Happiness Report 2018 — Chapter 2: "International Migration and World Happiness"
- Source: World Happiness Report (Helliwell, Layard, Sachs eds.)
- URL: https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR18_Ch02.pdf (summary at https://whatworkswellbeing.org/blog/world-happiness-report-2018-migration-wellbeing-and-happiness/)
- Finding: "If we rank countries according to the happiness of their immigrant populations, the results are almost exactly the same as for the rest of the population." Correlation between foreign-born and domestically-born happiness across 117 countries = **0.96**. Migrant acceptance increases happiness of **both** immigrants and the locally born.
- Methodology: Gallup World Poll data; Migrant Acceptance Index (three questions on acceptance across 138 countries).
- Relevance: The strongest evidence that immigrant happiness is determined by host-country quality of life, not origin. The Migrant Acceptance Index finding—that acceptance benefits both populations—is central to the happiness question.

### Gallup Migrant Acceptance Index (2016-2019)
- Source: Gallup World Poll
- URL: https://news.gallup.com/poll/320678/world-grows-less-accepting-migrants.aspx
- Finding: Global migrant acceptance declined from 5.34 to 5.21 (2016-2019). Most-accepting countries (2019): Canada (8.46), Iceland (8.41), New Zealand (8.32), Australia (8.28), US (7.95). Least-accepting: North Macedonia (1.49), Hungary (1.64), Serbia (1.79). Sharpest declines in Latin America after Venezuelan migration crisis: Peru fell from 6.33 to 3.61; Colombia from 6.13 to 3.98.
- Methodology: ~1,000 adults per country, 145 countries surveyed, three questions on increasing personal proximity to migrants.
- Relevance: Demonstrates that rapid, large-scale migration (Venezuelan crisis) can sharply reduce acceptance—and acceptance correlates with both immigrant and native happiness.

### Hainmueller & Hopkins (2014) — "Public Attitudes Toward Immigration"
- Source: Annual Review of Political Science
- URL: https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev-polisci-102512-194818
- Finding: Anti-immigrant sentiment is driven by **cultural-national identity concerns**, not personal economic anxiety. No evidence that natives oppose immigrants with skills similar to their own. In Swiss naturalization votes, immigrants from culturally distant countries were **40% less likely** to be approved, even with identical occupations and language skills.
- Methodology: Survey experiments, analysis of Swiss naturalization voting records.
- Relevance: Establishes that immigration attitudes (which mediate the happiness channel) are about cultural threat perception, not economic competition.

### Nikolova & Graham (2014) — "Does Moving Across International Borders Boost Migrants' Incomes, Happiness and Freedom Satisfaction?"
- Source: Brookings Institution
- URL: https://www.brookings.edu/articles/does-moving-across-international-borders-boost-migrants-incomes-happiness-and-freedom-satisfaction/
- Finding: Migrants gain ~1.0-1.2 points on a 0-10 life satisfaction scale; income premium of ~$21,000 international dollars; migrants from recent EU enlargements showed ~40% greater satisfaction with freedom to choose in life.
- Methodology: Gallup World Poll data, transition economies to advanced OECD nations.
- Relevance: Strong evidence that migration improves migrant wellbeing. Notes that "migration's benefits for destination economies outweigh the costs."

### Meta-analysis on Diversity and Social Trust
- Source: Utah State University graduate report (2023), synthesizing 87 studies with 1,001 estimates
- URL: https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1002&context=gradreports2023
- Finding: Ethnic diversity has a statistically significant **negative** relationship with social trust across 87 studies. However, the negative effects found in the US (Putnam) are weaker in European cross-national contexts.
- Relevance: Confirms a real but modest diversity-trust relationship, while highlighting that institutional context moderates the effect substantially.

## Key Debates

### 1. Does immigration reduce native happiness?
- **The null finding**: O'Connor (2020) finds no statistically significant effect across 28 EU countries over 27 years—the most comprehensive test. The WHR 2018 shows immigrant and native happiness rankings correlate at 0.96.
- **The conditional finding**: Brockmann & Fernandez-Urbano (2026) show it depends on immigrant gender composition. Net migration may decrease happiness only in already-happy countries (threshold effect).
- **The social trust channel**: Putnam (2007) shows diversity reduces trust short-term, but this has not been convincingly linked to measured life satisfaction declines in large-N studies.

### 2. Short run vs. long run
- Near-universal agreement that short-run adjustment costs exist (Putnam's "hunkering down," acceptance declines after Venezuelan crisis).
- Disagreement on whether long-run assimilation fully resolves these costs or whether some permanent social capital loss persists.
- Noah Smith and Putnam himself argue the long run favors new cross-cutting identities; skeptics argue this depends on speed and cultural distance.

### 3. What drives anti-immigration sentiment—economics or culture?
- Hainmueller & Hopkins: cultural identity threat, not economic competition, is the primary driver.
- Yglesias: loss-of-control perceptions, not measured economic harm.
- Cowen: both channels operate, but the political dilemma is structural regardless of cause.

### 4. Does acceptance mediate everything?
- WHR 2018 and Gallup data suggest acceptance is the key moderator—countries with higher acceptance see higher happiness for both immigrants and natives.
- But acceptance itself is endogenous to immigration levels and speed (see Latin American declines after Venezuelan crisis).
- This creates a potential feedback loop: rapid immigration reduces acceptance, reduced acceptance reduces happiness, which generates further anti-immigration sentiment.

### 5. Speed and composition matter more than level
- Emerging evidence (Brockmann 2026, Gallup acceptance trends, Yglesias on asylum vs. legal immigration) suggests the rate of change and composition of immigration flows matter more for native wellbeing than the absolute stock of immigrants.
- Countries with long histories of managed immigration (Canada, Australia) maintain high acceptance scores despite large immigrant populations.
