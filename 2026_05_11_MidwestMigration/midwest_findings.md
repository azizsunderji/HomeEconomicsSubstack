# Midwest Migration — Findings & Notes

*Assembled 2026-05-11. The story is about the **2021 → 2024 change**, when the post-COVID Sun Belt rebalancing reversed. The Midwest's improvement is real but not unique — it's part of a larger pattern affecting every region that was losing people to the South.*

---

## 1. The headline (2021 → 2024)

**The Midwest's "migration turnaround" is part of a broader post-COVID unwind. Everywhere that was losing people to the Sun Belt saw outflows collapse between 2021 and 2024.**

- Annual outflow from the **Midwest** fell from 940K → 815K (–125K). Inflow barely moved (785K → 764K). Net improved from –155K → –51K.
- Same pattern in the **Northeast**: outflow 908K → 780K (–128K). Inflow 594K → 559K (–35K). Net –314K → –220K.
- Same pattern in the **West**: outflow 1,143K → 984K (–159K). Inflow 912K → 835K (–77K). Net –231K → –148K.
- The mirror is the **South**: inflow 1,870K → 1,592K (–278K). Outflow basically flat (+3K). Net +700K → +419K.

So the story isn't "Midwest is winning." It's **"The Sun Belt boom unwound."** Three regions stopped bleeding to the South because the people fleeing to the South stopped fleeing. The Midwest's improvement is a third of a regional pattern, not a unique structural shift.

| Region | Net 2021 | Net 2024 | Δ Net | Mostly driven by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Midwest | –155K | –51K | +105K | Outflow –125K |
| Northeast | –314K | –220K | +93K | Outflow –128K |
| West | –231K | –148K | +82K | Outflow –159K |
| South | +700K | +419K | –280K | Inflow –278K |

The numbers are uncannily symmetric. The South's lost inflows (–278K) almost exactly match the combined outflow collapses of NE + MW + W (–412K, with about 130K of that going somewhere other than the South, presumably staying home).

**Likely drivers of the unwind**:
- The cost-of-living arbitrage trade that pulled families to the Sun Belt for 30 years just broke. Indianapolis, Nashville, Phoenix, Austin, Tampa, Charlotte — none are "cheap" anymore.
- Remote work removing the "must move for jobs" pressure.
- Post-COVID overall reduction in interstate moves — Americans are becoming more locked-in-place.
- Climate / insurance pressure on FL, TX coastal areas may be a small contributor (insurance costs in FL/CA pricing out marginal movers).

---

## 2. What's distinctive about the Midwest

Three things are notable about the Midwest's specific position in this rebalancing:

1. **Its starting net was less negative than NE or W**, so the same ~+100K improvement put it closer to break-even. In 2024 the Midwest's net (–51K) is by far the smallest deficit among the three "improving" regions. The Northeast and West are still losing ~150–220K/yr each.

2. **Six states do 91% of the regional improvement** between 2014–18 and 2021–24 (full-period view): Missouri, Ohio, Kansas, Michigan, Illinois, Indiana. North Dakota *worsened* (Bakken oil unwind). Nebraska, South Dakota, Iowa, Minnesota basically flat.

3. **Each state got there a different way**:
   - **Most** (Ohio, Missouri, Kansas, Michigan, ND) improved via fewer leaving.
   - **Illinois and Indiana** improved almost entirely via more arrivals (~+8K and +7K in inflow respectively, with outflow nearly flat).

The state-level decomposition chart (`outputs/midwest_state_decomposition.svg`) shows this clearly: green segments dominate for OH/MO/KS/MI/ND ("fewer leaving"); blue segments dominate for IL/IN ("more arriving").

## 4. Where Midwesterners go (and how that's changed)

Top destinations for people leaving the Midwest. The big movers between 2014–18 and 2021–24:

- **California**: 89K → 70K (–19K). The biggest single drop. Fewer Midwesterners chasing CA tech jobs.
- **Florida**: 123K → 130K (+8K). Still top destination, growing.
- **Texas**: 106K → 109K (+3K). Slight gain.
- **Colorado**: 50K → 42K (–8K). Denver/Boulder draw weakening.
- **Kentucky**: 42K → 36K (–5K). Neighbor-state pull weakening.
- **Tennessee**: 38K → 38K (flat). Nashville effect plateaued.
- **Washington**: 29K → 24K (–5K). Seattle softer.

The decline in CA/CO/WA destinations is consistent with the broader Sun Belt unwind — the marquee Western metros are less of a draw than they were.

## 5. Illinois deep-dive: who's leaving less, who's leaving more

Illinois alone is interesting because it shows the pattern in microcosm. Change in 2-year out-migration, 2014–15 → 2023–24:

**LEAVING LESS**:
- Children under 18: **–47K** (huge — fewer families with kids leaving)
- Lower-income (<$50K HH): **–104K** (enormous)
- 25–34 year-olds: –19K
- 50–64: –9K

**LEAVING MORE**:
- High-income ($200K+ HH): **+54K** (much more leaving)
- $100–200K: +18K
- 65+: +3K (slight retiree increase)
- 18–24: +5K (slight)

The "improvement" is really two opposite trends:
1. **Middle/working class stopped fleeing** (cost-of-living arbitrage to nearby Midwest no longer pays off — Indianapolis isn't cheap).
2. **Wealthy are leaving at accelerating rates** (likely tax flight to TN/FL/TX).

**Top destination shifts from Illinois** (change in 2-yr outflow):
- Indiana: **–20K** (way fewer leaving IL for Indiana)
- Iowa: –7K, Missouri: –8K
- Tennessee: **+6K** (Nashville draw growing)
- Florida, Texas, California: roughly flat

## 6. Brain drain is still very much a thing

Even with fewer people leaving the Midwest overall, the *quality* of leavers is unchanged: out-migrants consistently earn more and are better educated than arrivers, and the gap is structurally wider than it was 20 years ago.

**Median HH income, Midwest inter-regional flows, real 2024 dollars** (3-yr smoothed):

| Period | Leaver | Arriver | Gap | Leaver premium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2005–07 | $78.4K | $72.8K | +$5.6K | +8% |
| 2009–11 | $78.3K | $71.9K | +$6.4K | +9% |
| 2014–16 | $87.6K | $77.1K | +$10.5K | +14% |
| 2018–20 | $96.0K | $83.5K | +$12.5K | +15% |
| 2022–24 | $97.1K | $81.6K | +$15.6K | +19% |

Two things to notice:

1. **Both groups got richer.** Real-terms incomes for both arrivers and leavers rose ~$10–20K over 20 years. This isn't a story of impoverishment — it's a story of *selection*.

2. **The leaver premium roughly tripled.** From an ~8% gap in the late 2000s to ~19% in 2022–24. Around 2014–15 something structural shifted (Bakken-era was already over; this is the rise of remote knowledge work + persistent coastal-metro pull). The gap then stuck at the new higher level rather than reverting.

Single-year 2024 is an interesting wrinkle: gap narrowed to $9.7K (11%) as arrivers' median jumped to $85K. Could be noise (single-year ACS, post-COVID volatility) or a genuine sign of the unwind broadening to higher-income arrivers. Worth watching another year before calling it.

**Education**: Bachelor's-or-higher share of adult out-migrants is now ~57% vs ~51% for adult arrivers — gap has widened modestly from ~4pp (2005) to ~6pp (2024).

So the headline "fewer leaving" is real but specifically about lower-income leavers. Higher-skilled, higher-income residents are still net-leaving the region, and the income gap with arrivers is wider than at any point since 2005.

## 7. Natural change is collapsing, which makes migration matter more

Births and deaths in the Midwest, 2011 → 2025:

| Year | Natural change (births – deaths) |
|---|---|
| 2011 | +248K |
| 2014 | +240K |
| 2018 | +158K |
| 2021 | **–15K** (COVID) |
| 2022 | –18K |
| 2023 | +43K |
| 2024 | +50K |
| 2025 | +46K |

The collapse is roughly 50/50: births down ~100K vs 2011, deaths up ~100K. Both natural change and migration now sit in a ±50K band, where they used to be ±200K apart.

**Kicker**: a 50K swing in migration didn't matter when natural change was +250K. It matters now because natural change is +50K. Same migration improvement, very different consequences for population dynamics.

## 8. Caveats, methodology, cross-checks

### Caveats

- **2014–18 vs 2021–24 comparison includes the 2021–22 pandemic spike** (especially in the South, where 2021–22 net was +700K/yr — far above pre-COVID baseline). For cleaner reads use either 2014–18 vs 2023–24, or single-year 2021 vs 2024.
- **2020 is not surveyed** by ACS due to COVID — gaps in time series.
- **Intra-Midwest moves are excluded** in the headline charts (IL → WI doesn't count as either an inflow or outflow to "Midwest" as a region).
- **ACS vs PEP**: at the regional aggregate level they track each other within 1–2K for 2024. At state level the ACS has bigger margins of error.

### U-Haul / United Van Lines cross-check

U-Haul says the Midwest *worsened* 2024 → 2025; Ohio's 29-position drop is the biggest of any state. PEP says the Midwest *improved* to its first positive year. These contradict directionally.

The honest read: **U-Haul measures a different demographic slice** — DIY younger and less-affluent movers. PEP/ACS measures total population. If our brain-drain story is right (wealthy leaving more, lower-income leaving less), U-Haul should show the *opposite* of total population trends — and it does. So U-Haul is actually evidence FOR the brain-drain story, not a refutation of the migration story.

UVL 2024–2025 shows Illinois moving toward "balanced" — consistent with PEP.

### Aziz's framing notes

1. **Population dynamics are slow.** A 30-year inflection that nudges +16K is a real signal even if it's not 200K. Direction, not magnitude, matters when you're talking secular regional trends.

2. **The deeper trend isn't really about the Midwest — it's about American mobility collapsing nationwide.** The Midwest is a passive beneficiary of a rebalancing happening above its head, not an active draw.

3. **The cost-of-living-arbitrage trade just broke.** Indianapolis isn't cheap anymore. Nashville isn't cheap. Phoenix isn't cheap. That's the most durable structural change here.

4. **Gerli's directionally right but his framing overstates.** Data says "losing less," not "winning." Real difference for tax base, school enrollment, real estate — but very different from "winning."
